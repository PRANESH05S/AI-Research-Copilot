### 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

#### 2.1 Introduction & Theoretical Background
The literature review begins by establishing the theoretical foundation and context for the study, highlighting the critical role of AIoT (Artificial Intelligence of Things) in integrating digital technologies to enhance water quality monitoring. The concept of Water Quality Index (WQI) is introduced, along with its significance in assessing and managing water quality in both urban and rural settings. This context sets the stage for an in-depth analysis of the challenges and opportunities associated with integrating AIoT architectures for WQI forecasting.

#### 2.2 Evolution of Existing Approaches
Several studies have explored the integration of AIoT in water quality monitoring, with methodologies ranging from traditional machine learning (ML) to more advanced deep learning, transformer, and generative AI techniques. Traditional ML approaches, such as supervised and unsupervised learning, have been employed to predict WQI based on physical and chemical parameters (e.g., temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, electrical conductivity). Deep learning, in particular, has shown promise in complex data modeling and anomaly detection, though it often necessitates substantial computational resources. Transformers and generative AI methods have further expanded the scope of applications by enabling contextual understanding and data augmentation.

#### 2.3 Traditional & Classical Machine Learning Methods
Traditional machine learning methods have been widely used in water quality monitoring, with studies utilizing regression and classification algorithms to predict WQI based on a suite of physical parameters. These methods, while effective in certain scenarios, often struggle with data sparsity, especially in rural environments where data collection and management infrastructure is less robust. Supervised learning methods, such as support vector machines (SVM) and random forests, are particularly adept at handling labeled datasets, although they require extensive preprocessing and feature selection. Unsupervised learning approaches, such as clustering algorithms, are useful for anomaly detection but may not provide sufficient accuracy for high-stakes decision-making in regulatory contexts.

#### 2.4 Deep Learning, Transformer & Generative AI Methods
Recent advancements in deep learning have introduced new paradigms for WQI forecasting. Deep neural networks (DNNs), particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), have been employed to model complex spatial and temporal patterns in water quality data. These methods often require large datasets and significant computational power, which can be a limiting factor in resource-constrained settings. Transformer architectures, with their attention mechanisms, offer enhanced performance in tasks requiring fine-grained contextual information. Generative AI methods, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs), have been applied to generate synthetic datasets to augment real-world data, thereby improving the robustness of predictive models. However, these methods often require careful validation to ensure they do not introduce bias or misleading patterns.

#### 2.5 Synthesis & Comparative Matrix of Existing Research
A comparative matrix synthesizes the strengths and weaknesses of various approaches, illustrating the trade-offs between accuracy, computational efficiency, and data requirements. For instance, traditional ML methods are often more interpretable and less data-intensive, making them suitable for high-stakes regulatory applications. Deep learning, while offering superior performance in complex scenarios, requires extensive data and computational resources, which may be prohibitive in rural and resource-limited settings. Transformer and generative AI methods, despite their advantages in handling sparse and noisy data, can introduce biases if not carefully calibrated.

#### 2.6 Critical Limitations of Existing Systems
Despite the advancements, existing AIoT-based systems for WQI forecasting have been constrained by several limitations. Firstly, there is a persistent urban-centric bias in architectural designs, which often overlooks the unique challenges and data availability in rural areas. Secondly, the reliance on supervised learning methods for rural settings, where data scarcity is a significant issue, has led to suboptimal model performance and increased costs. Lastly, there is often a lack of alignment between AI analytics and regulatory indices, making it challenging to translate predictive insights into actionable regulatory decisions.

#### 2.7 Identified Research Gaps
The identified research gaps include the need for more robust and scalable AIoT architectures capable of handling diverse and sparse data in rural settings. There is also a need for more integrated and federated systems that can leverage data from multiple sources, such as point-source pollution, runoff, and domestic wastewater, to improve overall system robustness. Additionally, there is a critical need for more transparent and interpretable models that can be easily validated and aligned with regulatory frameworks. Lastly, there is a lack of comprehensive evaluation metrics and benchmarks that can reliably assess the performance and reliability of AIoT-based WQI forecasting systems.

#### 2.8 Chapter Summary
This chapter has critically evaluated the existing literature on AIoT-based digital monitoring architectures for WQI forecasting, highlighting the strengths and weaknesses of various methodologies. The findings underscore the need for more robust, scalable, and interpretable AIoT architectures that can effectively address the unique challenges of both urban and rural water quality monitoring. The synthesis of existing research provides a roadmap for future studies, emphasizing the importance of addressing the identified gaps to enhance the real-world applicability and effectiveness of AIoT-based solutions in water quality management.
